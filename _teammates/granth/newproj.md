An AI-powered app with cognitive games and memory-support tools, built specifically for elderly dementia patients in India's North-Eastern Region — in their own regional languages (Assamese, Khasi, Mizo, Manipuri, Bodo, and others), with themes and visuals that actually feel familiar to them, and designed to work even without reliable internet.

Why (the real problem)
Dementia is rising among the elderly in NER, but specialist neurologists, memory clinics, and cognitive therapy centers are scarce and far away in this region.
Every dementia-support app that exists today (Memory Lane Games, Lumosity, Ami, etc.) is built in English, for Western or East-Asian users — nothing exists for someone who only speaks Khasi or Assamese, has never used a smartphone before, and lives somewhere with patchy internet.
Family caregivers are often alone in managing this — no local support network, no tools in a language grandma actually understands, and constant worry about missed medicines or the patient wandering off or getting confused.
How (what it actually does)
Cognitive games — simple, adaptive games for memory, attention, and pattern recognition, using AI to make them easier or harder based on how the person is doing that day.
Familiar themes — instead of generic Western quiz content, games use NER-local imagery, sounds, and everyday scenarios (local festivals, familiar foods, regional folk elements) so it actually resonates and holds attention.
Voice-first, regional language interaction — a patient can speak instead of typing/reading, since literacy and comfort with screens can't be assumed.
Daily reminders — nudges for medicine, water, meals, and doctor's appointments, spoken aloud in their language.
Offline-first design — works and saves progress without internet, then syncs data whenever connectivity returns — critical for remote NER villages.
Caregiver dashboard — family members or health workers can see how the patient is doing over time (which games, how often, any decline in performance) and get alerted if something seems off.
Business model
Free for the patient and family — this should never be a paywalled product for someone already dealing with a hard diagnosis.
State health department licensing — NER states' health/social welfare departments adopt it as part of elderly care or NER-specific digital health initiatives, paying for deployment and maintenance.
NGO and CSR partnerships — organizations like Dementia India Alliance or ARDSI (who currently only offer helplines/pamphlets) could license or co-brand this as their first real interactive tool, funded via CSR grants focused on elderly/healthcare welfare in the North-East.
Community health worker integration — ASHA workers or local health volunteers could be trained to help onboard elderly users, turning this into a supported rollout rather than an app people have to figure out alone.
Later stage: could offer an anonymized, aggregated cognitive-decline data layer to researchers/public health bodies studying dementia trends in underserved regions — genuinely valuable data that doesn't exist today.
Who it's for
Primary user: elderly dementia/memory-loss patients in rural and semi-urban North-Eastern India
Secondary user: family caregivers, who get peace of mind and an early-warning system instead of guessing how their loved one is doing

[24/08/26, 10:04:09 PM] JainSuyogya: Game Categories

1.⁠ ⁠Memory Improvement

Match the pairs — flip cards to find matching images (local fruits, tools, animals, festival items instead of generic clip art)
What was here? — show a familiar scene (a kitchen, a market stall) for a few seconds, then ask what changed or what was missing
Face-name matching — using the patient's own family photos (uploaded by caregiver) so it's personally meaningful, not generic stock photos
Sequence recall — repeat a short sequence of sounds, colors, or objects shown in order

2.⁠ ⁠Attention & Concentration

Spot the difference — two nearly identical pictures of a local scene, find what's different
Find the object — locate a specific item hidden among clutter (like a specific vegetable in a market scene)
Simple sorting — sort objects by color, size, or type (e.g., separate fruits from vegetables)

3.⁠ ⁠Daily Routine Recall

"What comes next?" — show steps of a familiar routine (making tea, getting dressed) slightly out of order, ask the patient to arrange them correctly
Time-of-day matching — match an activity to the right time of day (breakfast → morning, prayer → evening, etc., adaptable to local customs)

4.⁠ ⁠Pattern & Object Recognition

Complete the pattern — simple visual sequences (colors, shapes) with one missing piece
Name the object — show a common regional object (a specific tool, food item, or craft) and have them identify or describe it
Odd one out — spot which item doesn't belong in a group

5.⁠ ⁠Emotional & Social Engagement

Music and reminiscence — play familiar regional folk songs and ask simple questions or just let them react/hum along (this is less "testing," more mood-lifting)
Storytelling prompts — show an old-style village scene or festival image and gently prompt them to talk about a memory (great for early-stage patients who can still converse)
Family photo review — a calm, guided walk through family photos with simple narration

[24/08/26, 10:04:11 PM] JainSuyogya: A few things worth knowing:

Not all dementia patients have the same speech ability. Early-stage patients often speak fairly normally, just with word-finding trouble or repetition. It's mid-to-late stage where speech becomes significantly harder, and by late stage, verbal communication may become minimal. So "dementia patients don't talk properly" isn't universally true — it's a spectrum, and your voice bot should adapt to where the person actually is, not assume the hardest case for everyone.

A voice bot's real value here isn't "having a conversation." It's better used as one-directional or very simple two-way interaction — the bot speaks a game instruction or a gentle reminder ("Have you had water today?"), and the patient responds with a very simple action (a tap, a single word, a yes/no, or nothing at all if they can't). Don't design it to expect fluent back-and-forth dialogue as the default.

Where it genuinely helps: reading out game instructions aloud (removes reliance on reading), gentle reminders spoken instead of a silent notification a confused person might not understand, and capturing whatever the patient can say (even just "yes," a hum, or a name) as a positive engagement signal rather than requiring full sentences.

Where it could actually cause harm/frustration: if it repeatedly asks open-ended questions and can't handle a patient's confused, incomplete, or repetitive answers gracefully, that's a bad, potentially distressing experience — for many dementia patients, repeated failure or being misunderstood by technology increases agitation, not engagement.

The safer design principle: make voice optional and supplementary, never the only way to interact. Every voice-based feature should have a touch/visual fallback, and the bot should be designed to gracefully accept "I don't know" or silence, not push for a better answer.
